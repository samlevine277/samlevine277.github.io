The Forklift and the Fundamentals: AI, Guidance, and Learning

January 16, 2026

In her blog post, Eliana Nodari examines the role of AI in education through Ted Chiang’s metaphor of AI as a “forklift in the weight room.” Her core concern is not that AI exists, but that it may allow students to bypass the mental effort required for genuine learning.

“Writing is thinking. If we outsource that thinking to AI, we risk losing the struggle that actually helps ideas form.” — Eliana Nodari

Nodari’s argument emphasizes that writing is not just a way to display knowledge, but the process through which knowledge is created. If AI performs the difficult parts of that process, students may produce polished work without developing the skills that writing is meant to teach.

Why This Concern Matters

This critique is especially persuasive in a Gen Z media environment defined by speed and constant consumption. Students are surrounded by smooth, confident language online, and AI-generated writing can blend seamlessly into that landscape. Nodari’s warning reminds us that fluency without understanding is not the same as learning.

I agree with Nodari that when AI is applied with no thinking at all, it is not educational. However, I want to extend her argument by questioning whether all assistance necessarily undermines learning.

When AI Becomes Guidance, Not a Shortcut

Many students do not turn to AI because they want to avoid thinking, but because they do not yet know how to begin. In these cases, AI can function less like a shortcut and more like a guide—helping students ask better questions, clarify confusion, and build foundational understanding.

Ethan Mollick, a professor who studies AI and education, makes this distinction clear in his book Co-Intelligence. He argues that the educational value of AI depends on how it is used.

“The danger is not using AI to help you think, but using AI instead of thinking.” — Ethan Mollick, Co-Intelligence

This framing helps refine the forklift metaphor. Using a forklift to avoid lifting entirely prevents growth. But using one to learn form, understand limits, or safely engage before strength develops can support learning rather than replace it.

Curiosity and Learning in a Media-Saturated World

In a media environment where information is consumed rapidly and often passively, AI can either reinforce disengagement or interrupt it. When students use AI to ask questions, test ideas, or explore topics they do not yet understand, the tool can encourage curiosity rather than eliminate effort.

The key distinction is intent. If AI supplies answers students could generate themselves, it undermines learning. But when it supports curiosity and guidance, it can help students build the fundamentals necessary for deeper thinking.

Conclusion

Eliana Nodari is right to caution against AI use that removes struggle and judgment from education. Writing should remain a space where thinking happens. At the same time, thoughtful use of AI can help students learn how to think when they are still developing those skills.

The challenge is not whether students use the forklift, but whether they are learning how to lift. When AI supports curiosity instead of replacing thought, it can become an educational tool rather than an intellectual shortcut.
