AI in Sports: Tool, Not Replacement

In his blog post, Dominic Debro raises some important concerns about AI in sports. He argues that safety algorithms and wearable monitoring can turn athletes into passive actors, overly reliant on data, and possibly weaken their autonomy and resilience. While I see where he’s coming from, I disagree with the idea that AI reduces effort or replaces the human drive that defines athletic performance.

What Dominic Debro Argues

Debro worries that AI monitoring isn’t just about keeping athletes safe — it’s a form of surveillance that can extend to workplaces and daily life. He writes:

“…employee might be passed over ... because an AI flagged their ‘engagement levels’ as declining” and that “Safety becomes a pretext for 24/7 surveillance.”

His main point is that relying on AI too much could weaken personal agency and reduce resilience, potentially leading athletes and even workers to act more like data points than humans.

Why I See AI Differently

From my perspective as an athlete, AI is a tool — one of many — rather than a replacement for effort. Structured, data-driven training has always been part of sports. Reviewing game footage, practicing plays, and following training plans are essentially forms of algorithmic thinking. In a way, athletes have been following algorithms long before AI became prominent. AI simply expands on that, providing new insights without replacing the human decision-making that drives motivation, grit, and performance.

For example, athletic trainers (ATs) take AI data and combine it with their knowledge of the athlete’s body, mindset, and fatigue levels. According to a 2022 study published in the Journal of Athletic Training:

“Athletic trainers integrate objective data with subjective clinical assessment to make safe return-to-play decisions, acknowledging that metrics alone cannot define readiness.”

This shows that AI alone doesn’t dictate decisions — human expertise is still essential. Even if AI flags a high-risk score, it’s the AT or coach who interprets the data, performs functional tests, and decides the best course of action. This human element ensures that athletes remain active participants in their training and performance decisions.

AI Does Not Reduce Effort

Some argue that athletes might work less if AI tells them they’re at risk. I strongly disagree. AI is feedback, not a directive. Athletes still have to choose how to respond to that feedback. Pushing through challenges, deciding to train harder, and testing limits are all personal choices that AI cannot replace. AI doesn’t change who the athlete is or how they think — the human mind and personal drive still dominate performance.

Research supports this perspective. A recent article from MDPI, Empowering the Sports Scientist with Artificial Intelligence in Training, Performance, and Health Management, notes:

“While AI systems can process vast amounts of data, they often struggle with the unpredictable nature of sports… Therefore, sports scientists must strike a balance between relying on AI for data-driven insights and applying their expertise to make informed decisions.”

In other words, AI provides information, but humans interpret it and make the final decisions. Just like reviewing film or practicing drills, AI is an added resource — not a replacement for human judgment or effort.

AI as a Tool, Not a Crutch

Some of Debro’s concerns focus on the idea that AI could make athletes passive or overly reliant on technology. I understand this worry, but I think it oversimplifies how athletes interact with data. Using AI does not mean an athlete stops thinking critically. Watching film, practicing specific plays, and tracking stats have been part of sports for decades — essentially acting as manual “algorithms” for learning. AI simply automates some of that analysis and provides additional insight. It doesn’t replace the mental effort, grit, or decision-making inherent in the sport.

In my experience, AI insights never tell me how hard to push or how much effort to give. That choice is entirely personal. Even if AI says I’m at risk, I still train, compete, and push myself. The tools available, including ATs, coaches, and performance specialists, ensure the human perspective always guides decision-making. AI adds context, but it doesn’t dictate effort or ambition.

AI Can Enhance Resilience

Debro worries that AI might reduce grit and resilience. From my perspective, it can enhance resilience. AI highlights patterns in fatigue, performance, and recovery, which allows athletes to train smarter while still challenging themselves. Understanding your body and optimizing recovery doesn’t weaken determination — it strengthens long-term performance. You still face competition, split-second decisions, and unpredictable challenges. AI doesn’t remove those experiences; it provides better guidance on how to approach them strategically.

Another perspective comes from a 2023 study in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living:

“AI-driven performance monitoring enhances athletes’ ability to manage workload and prevent overtraining, but it is the athlete’s decision-making and discipline that determine ultimate performance outcomes.”

This supports my point: AI is a resource, not a replacement for effort or resilience.

Conclusion

AI should be seen as a decision-support tool in sports, not a replacement for human drive or effort. While Dominic Debro raises valid concerns about surveillance and agency, my experience shows that AI complements human judgment and does not diminish motivation. Coaches, athletic trainers, and athletes themselves use AI alongside intuition, experience, and personal goals to make informed decisions.

Personally, AI insights don’t dictate my effort — they enhance it. Using AI responsibly helps athletes train smarter, recover faster, and perform better, while human agency and resilience remain central. Saying that athletes work less because of AI ignores the long history of strategic, data-informed training in sports. Watching film, practicing plays, and following drills have always been algorithmic in nature. AI just takes what athletes already do to the next level, offering more precise feedback without replacing the human element.

In 2026 and beyond, the goal is not to let AI replace human effort, but to empower athletes to reach their full potential safely and effectively. As an athlete, I can confidently say that my determination, work ethic, and personal drive remain unchanged by technology — AI is a tool, not a master.
