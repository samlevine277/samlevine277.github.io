AI in Sports: Tool, Not Replacement

In his blog post, Dominic Debro raises some important concerns about AI in sports. He argues that safety algorithms and wearable monitoring can turn athletes into passive actors, overly reliant on data, and possibly weaken their autonomy and resilience. While I see where he’s coming from, I disagree with the idea that AI reduces effort or replaces the human drive that defines athletic performance.

What Dominic Debro Argues

Debro worries that AI monitoring isn’t just about keeping athletes safe — it’s a form of surveillance that can extend to workplaces and daily life. He writes:

“...employee might be passed over ... because an AI flagged their ‘engagement levels’ as declining” and that “Safety becomes a pretext for 24/7 surveillance.”

His main point is that relying on AI too much could weaken personal agency and reduce resilience, potentially leading athletes and even workers to act more like data points than humans.

Why I See AI Differently

From my perspective as an athlete, AI is a tool — one of many — rather than a replacement for effort. Structured, data-driven training has always been part of sports. Reviewing game footage, practicing plays, and following training plans are essentially forms of algorithmic thinking. AI simply expands on that, providing new insights without replacing the human decision-making that drives motivation, grit, and performance.

Some argue that AI monitoring might make athletes rely on algorithms too much. I disagree with this statement. In reality, athletes have always used data and structured methods to improve. Practicing plays, reviewing film, and analyzing performance can be seen as an algorithmic approach that existed long before AI. This isn’t a new phenomenon. AI simply provides more detailed insights, but the athlete still decides how to act on them.

Athletic trainers (ATs) are a perfect example. They take AI data and combine it with their knowledge of the athlete’s body, mindset, and fatigue levels. According to a 2022 study published in the Journal of Athletic Training:

“Athletic trainers integrate objective data with subjective clinical assessment to make safe return-to-play decisions, acknowledging that metrics alone cannot define readiness.”

This shows that AI alone doesn’t dictate decisions — human expertise is still essential. There are many human aspects to training, recovery, and performance that AI cannot replace. The athlete, the coach, and the AT are the decision-makers — AI is a tool that informs them.

AI Does Not Reduce Effort

Some argue that athletes might work less if AI tells them they’re at risk. But the reality is that AI is just feedback. Athletes still have to choose how to respond to that feedback. Pushing through challenges, deciding to train harder, and testing limits are all personal choices that AI cannot replace.

Research supports this perspective. A recent article from MDPI, “Empowering the Sports Scientist with Artificial Intelligence in Training, Performance, and Health Management,” notes:

“While AI systems can process vast amounts of data, they often struggle with the unpredictable nature of sports… Therefore, sports scientists must strike a balance between relying on AI for data-driven insights and applying their expertise to make informed decisions.”

This means AI provides information, but humans interpret it and make the final decisions. As an athlete myself, what AI recommends doesn’t make me stop working hard — it guides me to make smarter choices. Human agency and effort are still central.

Human-Centered Technology

In practice, AI tools like wearables, heart rate monitors, and motion sensors give athletes and coaches real-time insights into performance and recovery. But these tools do not replace judgment. If an AI flags a high risk of fatigue, an athletic trainer might perform additional functional tests or talk with the athlete about how they feel. AI informs — humans decide.

Even when AI might suggest benching a player, there are human checks in place. The athletic trainer might conduct their own tests, consider the athlete’s mental state, and collaborate with coaches. AI does not change the athlete’s mindset or drive — the athlete’s motivation and work ethic remain personal decisions.

AI Can Enhance Resilience

Debro worries that AI might reduce grit. I see the opposite: AI can actually enhance resilience. By highlighting patterns in fatigue or performance, AI allows athletes to train smarter while still pushing their limits. Understanding one’s body and optimizing recovery doesn’t weaken determination — it strengthens long-term performance and safety.

Even in high-pressure sports, unpredictability remains. Athletes still make split-second decisions, face competition, and experience real-world challenges. AI does not remove these experiences; it provides better guidance to approach them strategically.

AI is a tool that complements training. According to an article from MDPI:

“AI empowers sports scientists to enhance athlete performance and health management without replacing human intuition and experience.”

This reinforces the idea that AI supports, but does not replace, the human aspects of sports.

Conclusion

AI should be seen as a decision-support tool in sports, not a replacement for human drive or effort. While Dominic Debro raises important concerns about surveillance and agency, my experience shows that AI complements human judgment and does not diminish motivation. Coaches, athletic trainers, and athletes themselves use AI alongside intuition, experience, and personal goals to make informed decisions.

For me, AI insights don’t dictate effort — they enhance it. When used responsibly, AI helps athletes train smarter, recover faster, and perform better, while human agency and resilience remain central. In 2026 and beyond, the goal is not to let AI replace human effort, but to empower athletes to reach their full potential safely and effectively. AI is just another resource — alongside ATs, coaches, and personal dedication — helping athletes make the most of their abilities.
